Structure of Chromosome & DNA

Chromosome Organization

A chromosome is made of DNA + proteins. The long thread of DNA wraps around proteins (histone proteins), forming nucleosomes, which then further coil up to form chromatin thread. Chromatin thread further condenses to form chromosomes seen in cell division under a microscope.

Significance of this organization:
  • Allows the long chain of DNA to be packed densely.
  • DNA molecules are protected by the proteins.
  • Allows accurate sorting of chromosomes during cell division without tangling.

DNA Structure Details

  • Blue bands: Represent the two sugar-phosphate chains.
  • Horizontal connections: Pairs of bases connecting the two chains.
  • The two chains run in opposite directions (antiparallel).
  • Dimensions: 3.4 nm per turn, 0.34 nm between bases, 2 nm width.

Worked Example 1: Base Composition

If a sample of DNA had 18% of guanine nucleotides, what percentage of its nucleotides would be thymine?

Solution: G pairs with C, so C = 18%. Total G + C = 36%. Remaining A + T = 64%. Since A = T, Thymine = 32%.

Relationship between Gene Expression and Protein Synthesis

Properties of the Genetic Code

  • Triplet Code: Three consecutive bases form a coding unit (codon) to specify one particular amino acid.
  • Start Codon: AUG codes for amino acid Met (methionine). It starts the process of protein synthesis.
  • Degenerate Code: A single amino acid may be coded by more than one triplet. Since 4 nucleotides form a triplet, there are 4³ = 64 combinations for only 20 amino acids.
  • Non-overlapping: Once bases are assigned to form a codon, they are not shared with other bases to form another codon.
  • Universal Code: The same triplet code works for the same amino acids in all organisms.
  • Nonsense Triplets: Do not code for any known amino acid; serve as stop codons (e.g., UAA, UAG, UGA).

Recombinant DNA Technology

Core Steps in Genetic Engineering

Isolate desired gene and cut plasmid

  • Use a restriction enzyme to isolate the desired gene from the source.
  • Use the same restriction enzyme to cut the plasmid for insertion.
  • This produces the same sticky ends on the target gene and the plasmid so they can be ligated together by complementary base pairing.

Join the DNA

  • Use DNA ligase to join the desired gene and cut plasmid.
  • The enzyme joins the cut ends of the DNA and the plasmid.

Introduce into host cells

  • Introduce the recombinant plasmids into bacterial cells.
  • Methods: Treatment with ice-cold calcium chloride (CaCl₂) solution followed by brief incubation at 40°C, or treatment with brief electric shock.

Selection & Mass Culture

  • Select transformed bacteria by culturing on nutrient agar containing a specific antibiotic (bacteria with plasmid have resistance gene).
  • Mass culture the transformed bacterial cells in a nutrient medium containing the antibiotic.
  • Induce expression of the gene encoding the protein product.
  • Extract and purify the gene product (e.g., human insulin).

Application: Human Insulin Production

Mass production of insulin to meet increasing demand instead of using pig insulin.

Benefits:

  • Helps diabetic patients to control the disease.
  • Eliminates the chance of transmission of animal pathogens.
  • Eliminates possible allergens from pig insulin (less side-effects).
  • Eliminates possible immune response produced by diabetic patients against pig insulin since the amino acid sequence is identical to human insulin.

Worked Example 2: Plasmid & Restriction Enzymes

A plasmid has two antibiotic resistance genes (ampicillin and tetracycline) and cut sites for enzymes P, Q, R. A student inserts a DNA fragment into the plasmid and transforms bacteria.

  • (a) Choose restriction enzyme: Use enzyme R. Its cut site is found at both ends of the DNA fragment, producing complementary sticky ends for insertion.
  • (b) Importance of ampicillin agar: To select bacteria that contain the plasmid (transformed), as they possess the ampicillin resistance gene and can survive.
  • (c) Why some have/don't have tetracycline resistance: Insertion of the DNA fragment interrupts the tetracycline resistance gene. Bacteria with successful insertion lose tetracycline resistance. Those with self-ligated plasmid (no insertion) retain it.

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Interactive PCR Cycle

PCR is used to amplify a small amount of DNA into a large amount for analysis.

95°C
1. Denaturation
High temperature breaks hydrogen bonds between the two strands of double helix DNA. Separates into two single strands.
~55°C
2. Annealing
Temperature is lowered. Primers bind to complementary sequences on the single-stranded DNA.
72°C
3. Extension
DNA polymerase adds free nucleotides (dNTPs) to the 3' end of the primers to synthesize new DNA.

Key Components:

  • Taq DNA polymerase: Heat-stable enzyme (from Thermus aquaticus) used so it doesn't denature at 95°C.
  • Primers: Short single-stranded DNA sequences. Two types are added because the DNA consists of two complementary strands with different sequences.
  • Free nucleotides (dNTPs): Building blocks for new DNA.

Graph Analysis:

  • Cycles 1-16: Exponential growth, doubling of DNA each cycle.
  • Cycles 17-20: Levelling out because nucleotides and primers are used up, and polymerase loses activity.

DNA Fingerprinting & Gel Electrophoresis

Principles

  • VNTRs (Variable Number Tandem Repeats): Short sequences of repeated DNA in the non-coding region. The number of VNTRs varies greatly from person to person.
  • RFLP (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism): Restriction enzymes break DNA into fragments of different lengths. This polymorphism is the original analysis process for DNA fingerprinting.

Worked Example 9: Family DNA Fingerprint

Child 2 is from the mother's previous marriage because some of their bands resemble the mother's, while other bands cannot be found in the father's pattern.

Why do biological children have different patterns? Due to random fertilization, independent assortment, and crossing over during meiosis. Each gamete bears different copies of chromosomes from parents.

Gene Therapy

Treatment of genetic diseases by substituting the function of a defective gene with a therapeutic (normal) gene.

In Vivo Gene Therapy

  • Therapeutic gene inserted into a vector (e.g., virus).
  • Viral vector introduced into patient's body.
  • Gene inserted into target cells (e.g., liver cells).
  • Expression produces correct protein.

Ex Vivo Gene Therapy

  • Therapeutic gene inserted into vector.
  • Target cells removed from patient and grown in culture.
  • Viruses added to insert therapeutic gene.
  • Transfected cells transplanted back to patient.

Case Study: SCID (Severe Combined Immunodeficiency)

  • Rare autosomal recessive disease caused by gene mutation rendering person unable to produce lymphocytes.
  • Cells from bone marrow (stem cells) are used instead of white blood cells.
  • Working gene inserted into stem cells -> cell divides -> produces white blood cells with working gene -> long-term cure.
  • If inserted into mature white blood cells, the working gene is lost after cell death.
  • Hazard: In 2002, 3 out of 11 SCID children got leukemia. The virus randomly inserted the gene into the genome, potentially affecting DNA sequences and gene expression.

Stem Cell Therapy

Nature of Stem Cells

  • Unspecialized cells (not specialized for any particular function).
  • Capable of dividing indefinitely by mitotic cell division to produce more stem cells.
  • Capable of differentiating into different types of specialized cells.

Worked Example 18: COVID-19 Treatment

Procedure: Umbilical cord blood collected -> Stem cells extracted and cultured -> Transferred into patient's lungs -> Damaged epithelium regenerates.

Why it works: Stem cells are cultured to increase number, differentiated into epithelium stem cells, introduced to lungs, proliferate and differentiate to repair damage.

Why immune response may occur: Stem cells are from another individual, carrying antigens different from the patient. Immune system may recognize as foreign.

Transgenic Organisms

Methods of Gene Transfer

Agrobacterium tumefaciens

  • The plasmid of soil bacterium combines with desired gene to form a recombinant plasmid.
  • Recombinant plasmid introduced into Agrobacterium, which infects crop plant cells easily.
  • Transformed plant cells selected in sterile culture medium -> develop into adult plants.
  • Limitations: Insertion is random (may affect expression). Target gene may not be expressed. Only a small proportion of cells infected. May have restrictions to infect certain hosts.

Gene Gun

  • Uses small metal particles coated with DNA, blasted at plant/animal tissue at high velocity.
  • Advantages: Direct introduction, doesn't need recombinant plasmid.
  • Disadvantages: Random insertion (success/expression not guaranteed). May cause mechanical damage. Requires costly devices (gene gun, gold particles).

Applications of Transgenic Plants

  • Pest resistance: BT gene produces BT toxin to kill pests.
  • Herbicide tolerance: HR gene allows crops to withstand herbicides.
  • Cold/drought tolerance: Increases farmland usability.
  • Nutritional content: GM rice produces carotene (Vitamin A precursor) to reduce night blindness.
  • Disease resistance: Produce anti-viral/fungal proteins.
  • Extended shelf life: Gene switches off ripening hormone production.

Cloning

Animal Cloning (e.g., Dolly the Sheep)

  1. Remove nucleus from an unfertilized egg cell (from another sheep).
  2. Fuse the enucleated egg cell with a donor cell (e.g., udder cell) using an electric spark.
  3. Embryo transformed into the womb of a surrogate mother.
  4. Surrogate gives birth. The cloned animal is genetically identical to the white-faced sheep that donated the udder cell.

Plant Cloning (Tissue Culture)

  1. Remove meristematic cells from parent plant.
  2. Put into sterile culture medium with nutrients.
  3. Cells grow by mitosis to form calli (undifferentiated mass).
  4. Transfer cells to medium with plant hormones to stimulate roots and shoots (plantlets).
  5. Transfer plantlets to soil.

Limitations:

  • Not applicable to all species.
  • Low success rate, high cost (especially animals).
  • Cloned animals have very short lifespans.
  • Human cloning is illegal (ethical issues, eugenics, loss of dignity, embryo wastage).

Monoclonal Antibodies

Production (Hybridoma Technology)

  1. Inject desired antigen into a mouse. Stimulates B cells to produce specific antibodies.
  2. Remove B cells (short-lived) from the spleen.
  3. Fuse B cells with tumour cells (long-lived) to form hybridomas.
  4. Hybridomas divide indefinitely and secrete large amounts of monoclonal antibodies.
  5. Screen in selective medium and culture.

Applications

  • Pregnancy Test: hCG in urine binds to mobile antibodies -> moves up -> binds to immobilized anti-hCG -> enzyme-linked antibody causes colour change.
  • Cancer Treatment (e.g., Trastuzumab): Binds to HER-2 receptors (found in large numbers on breast cancer cells). Blocks hormones from triggering cell division. Slows cancer development.
  • Drug Delivery: Antibodies bind specifically to cancer cells, delivering drugs directly. Lower dose needed, fewer side effects.
  • Organ Transplant: Antibodies attack T cells that cause rejection.

Bioethics

Ethical issues brought about by modern biotechnology.

GM Food and Organisms

  • May contain foreign genes forbidden by some religions.
  • Violation of natural organisms' intrinsic values.
  • Long-term effects unknown.
  • Antibiotic-resistance marker genes may transfer to pathogens ("superbugs").
  • Environmental: Gene pollution via cross-pollination. GM plants may outcompete wild species, upsetting ecological balance.

Cloning & Gene Therapy

  • Wastage of embryos during cloning.
  • Cloning promotes a 'uniparent' trend, against the natural 'biparent' society.
  • Gene therapy on germ cells causes irreversible changes to future generations. Who has the right to choose?
  • Gene replacement (e.g., IQ, skin colour) raises eugenics and racial issues.
